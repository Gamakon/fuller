\begin{abstract}
\texttt{fuller} is an e-graph engine that makes symbolic expressions smaller,
provably without changing what they compute. It is named for Buckminster
Fuller's \emph{ephemeralization} --- doing more with less --- applied to
equations. It was built to replace SymPy in the inner loop of a
symbolic-regression engine, where SymPy's complex-domain assumptions, native
signal handling and exponential simplification paths made expression cleanup
unreliable. The engine's distinguishing claim is a clean seam: a bounded
equality-saturation pass decides what is \emph{smaller}, and the training data
decides what is \emph{still correct}. That seam is what licenses simplification
as a genetic \emph{operator} rather than post-processing --- a Lamarckian
mechanism made sound by proof of equivalence. This paper is a system sketch. The
engine is implemented and in use --- datatype and guard relations, family-split
rulesets, the data-gated \texttt{denoise} operator, karva re-encoding, the
\texttt{snap}/\texttt{concretize} pair, physics-prior generation, a forked
multi-objective extractor, and a second target (Brainfuck) where equivalence is
decided exactly rather than statistically. What is \emph{not} established is
whether in-loop Lamarckian rewriting improves symbolic regression: the effect
observed is surprising and its sign is unknown. We state plainly what is built,
what is not, and which experiments would settle it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Physical laws are compact. Expressions produced by evolutionary search are not.
They arrive with constants absorbed into coefficients --- a fitted $0.0796$
concealing $1/(4\pi)$ --- with redundant structure such as $x\cdot 1 + 0\cdot y$,
with $\mathrm{Abs}(a^{1.5})$ where the domain is already positive, and with noise
terms that fit the sample rather than the underlying relationship. Much of the
gap between an expression that \emph{fits the data} and the \emph{law itself} is
this excess structure. Removing it is not cosmetic: a reported law is only
recognisable as a law once the excess is gone.

The obvious tool is a computer-algebra system, and in the host engine that meant
SymPy. Three properties make it a poor fit for a hot inner loop, where the
simplifier runs on every offspring of every generation. \emph{Domain mismatch}:
SymPy reasons over the complex domain by default, so simplifying a real-valued
gene legitimately produces $\mathrm{re}()$, $\mathrm{im}()$ and $\sinh()$ terms
the host must detect and reject --- a filtering dance rather than a result.
\emph{Unbounded cost}: \texttt{simplify()} has no iteration budget, and evolved
genes are adversarial by construction --- deep, redundant, full of near-cancelling
subtrees --- so its rewrite paths can blow up, with no principled way to ask for
\emph{a bounded amount} of simplification. \emph{Process hazard}: SymPy's native
components install their own signal handlers, so a long simplification in a
worker is not reliably interruptible, and a crash takes the run rather than the
call.

An e-graph is a better substrate. Equality saturation represents \emph{all}
equivalent forms at once, applies a rule schedule that can be explicitly capped,
extracts by an explicit cost model, and is deterministic: the same input always
yields the same output. Equivalence is \emph{proved} by saturation, not sampled.
\texttt{fuller} is a Rust crate over \texttt{egglog}~\cite{egglog}, exposed to
Python via PyO3, in the same engine family as Herbie~\cite{herbie}.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{A real-domain datatype [implemented].} The shared substrate is a
\texttt{Math} datatype declared in \texttt{egglog} surface syntax
(\texttt{src/expr.rs:29--63}). It carries \texttt{Num}, \texttt{Var}, the
arithmetic and transcendental operators, and the fixed powers \texttt{Pow2},
\texttt{Pow3}, \texttt{Inv}. The complex domain is deliberately excluded --- that
exclusion \emph{is} the design, and it is why the $\mathrm{re}()/\mathrm{im}()$
rejection problem of Section~\ref{sec:intro} has no analogue here.

\paragraph{Protected operators are distinct functions [implemented].} A
symbolic-regression primitive set uses guarded operators: protected division
returns $0$ on a zero denominator, protected $\log$ takes $\log|x|$. These are
declared as \emph{separate constructors}, not aliased to their raw counterparts
(\texttt{src/expr.rs:49--62}), because mapping \texttt{protected\_div} onto
\texttt{Div} would be unsound --- the two disagree exactly where it matters, at
zero and on negatives. Being distinct functions, no rewrite can substitute one
for the other: they are inert by default, and a rule fires on a protected form
only where it is provably sound for that form.

\paragraph{Domain facts as relations [implemented].} Many useful identities are
conditional: $\log(e^x)\to x$ needs a positive argument, and cancelling a
denominator needs it nonzero. \texttt{egglog} 2.0 cannot guard on the sign of a
free variable, so \texttt{fuller} carries domain knowledge as relations,
\texttt{(is-positive m)} and \texttt{(is-nonzero m)}, which a rule requires via
\texttt{:when} (\texttt{src/expr.rs:66--113}). Facts enter by propagation ---
$\mathrm{Exp}\,x$ is positive on all reals; a product or quotient of positives is
positive; positive implies nonzero --- or by the caller asserting a fact for a
specific node when the engine knows a variable's domain. A bare free variable is
\emph{not} assumed nonzero, so guarded rules conservatively do not fire on it.
That is the sound default: a missing fact costs a simplification, never
correctness. (One sharp edge: \texttt{egglog} runs only named rulesets a schedule
mentions, so a schedule omitting \texttt{guards} silently derives no facts.)

\paragraph{Rulesets split by family, because they are non-confluent
[implemented, measured].} The rule library (\texttt{src/ruleset/}) is partitioned
into \texttt{identities} (algebra), \texttt{powers}, \texttt{distribute},
\texttt{rational}, \texttt{trig}, \texttt{trig\_fu}, \texttt{sympy\_mined} and
\texttt{wide}. The partition is not tidiness. Distribution and the trigonometric
expansions each terminate \emph{alone} but explode the e-graph when co-saturated,
since both grow the graph without repeating; distribute and rational ping-pong the
same way (\texttt{src/parity.rs:53--68}). This was measured, not anticipated ---
runs pegged the CPU and had to be killed. The engine therefore selects one family
per task, and every schedule is a bounded \texttt{repeat}, never an unbounded
\texttt{saturate} (\texttt{src/extract.rs:31--37}).

\paragraph{The seam [implemented].} The core operator is \texttt{denoise}
(\texttt{src/extract.rs:8--17}):

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item saturate the algebra ruleset on the input term, bounded;
\item \texttt{extract\_variants($K$)} --- the $K$ lowest-structural-cost
      equivalent forms;
\item evaluate each candidate on the training rows;
\item return the smallest-cost candidate whose $R^2$ against the original is
      within \texttt{tolerance}; if none qualify, return the input
      \emph{unchanged}.
\end{enumerate}

The structural cost model decides \emph{``smallest''}; the data decides
\emph{``still correct''}. The two concerns stay cleanly separated, and that seam
is the paper's central design claim. It keeps the e-graph deterministic and
data-free, which is what makes it bounded and reproducible, while data-awareness
lives entirely outside it as an acceptance test. Two further guards make the
operator safe to call blindly: non-shrinking forms are never accepted
(\texttt{src/extract.rs:516}), so a rewrite cannot trade size for anything, and
the $R^2$ loss gate (\texttt{src/extract.rs:538}) rejects a cost-preferred
candidate whose behaviour moved. The API never raises: a pathological input
returns the input. A hard depth cap (\texttt{MAX\_EXPR\_DEPTH} $=256$,
\texttt{src/lib.rs:27}) bounds every recursive walk so an oversized gene surfaces
as an ordinary error rather than a stack overflow, which no catch could recover.

\section{Extraction as a Tournament}
\label{sec:extraction}

Equality saturation yields an equivalence class: forms that all denote the same
function. Equivalence holds in the \emph{goal} domain, so no member is preferred
on behaviour, and selecting one --- extraction --- is a tournament decided
entirely on properties of \emph{form}. The companion
paper~\cite{morgan2026tournaments} is the full treatment; this section states only
what \texttt{fuller} implements.

Stock e-graph extraction requires an \emph{additive, monotone} cost model, because
it is bottom-up dynamic programming: a term must cost no less than its subterms so
the walk never reconsiders a settled class, which forces every criterion into one
additive number. \texttt{fuller} vendors a forked extractor,
\texttt{hff\_extract} (\texttt{vendor-egglog/src/extract.rs:636--662}), which
instead enumerates each class's members under a bound and selects the one
minimising a caller-supplied \texttt{score(\&TermDag, TermId) -> f64} evaluated on
the \emph{whole} candidate term. No monotonicity is assumed, so a larger term may
legitimately score better --- impossible in the stock extractor. The e-graph
licenses this: every candidate is already provably equivalent, so the ranking may
be arbitrary without risking correctness.

\paragraph{The scorer, as implemented.} Form measures are a data-driven library
of \emph{(pattern, measure)} rules: the pattern decides whether the rule fires,
and a firing rule contributes one bounded component in $[0,1]$ with $0$ best, so
adding an objective is adding a rule (\texttt{src/score.rs:4--16}). The
projection is HFF-TrueNorth, and one detail is load-bearing: \textbf{the north
pole is always zeros} --- the origin of objective space, lifted to the augmented
sphere as $(0,\dots,0,1)$, which is pure minimisation. The ``balanced'' pole
$(1/\sqrt{m},\dots,1/\sqrt{m})$ is banned: it rewards equal trade-offs rather
than minimisation, and does not work in practice (the HFF crate states this at
\texttt{core\_functions.rs:105--109}). Every candidate is scored at one
\emph{fixed} dimension $k$, the full rule count, with a non-firing rule padded to
the neutral $0.5$; at fixed $k$ the incomplete-beta CDF correction is only a
monotone reshape, so it is omitted and the raw angle ranked
(\texttt{src/score.rs:284--331}). Cross-dimensionality CDF correction remains
valid in HFF's original fixed-objective population setting, but \emph{not} for
ranking variable-defect members of one e-class, where it inverts the order by
rewarding a form for firing more measures; the companion paper reports that
correction in full.

\paragraph{Two extraction paths coexist [implemented].} This should not be
overstated. The live \texttt{denoise} operator imports the \emph{stock}
scalar-cost extractor (\texttt{src/extract.rs:19}); what makes it safe is the
data gate, not the scorer. The HFF tournament is a separate path, exposed as
\texttt{eclass\_extract\_hff\_instrumented}, currently used for final-answer
tidying and for the tournament figures. Both being present makes the comparison
in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} cheap to run.

\section{Simplification as a Genetic Operator}
\label{sec:operator}

This is the contribution.

Classical GP treats simplification as post-processing, and for a good reason:
an unsound rewrite inside the loop corrupts the population, and the corruption is
heritable. \texttt{fuller}'s rewrites are either exact algebraic identities or
data-gated, which removes that constraint. Simplification can then act as a
genetic \emph{operator}, and a simplification once found is propagated by
crossover and judged by selection --- a Lamarckian mechanism made sound by proof
of equivalence.

\paragraph{The structural obstacle.} A simplified gene is usually \emph{shorter}
than the gene it replaces. In tree-GP that is a differently-shaped genome, which
breaks the genetic operators and uniform-length mating. Re-injection would
require variable-length genomes, and simplification would revert to
post-processing.

\paragraph{The ORF argument [implemented].} Gene expression
programming~\cite{ferreira2001} resolves this. A karva chromosome is a
fixed-length head followed by a fixed-length tail, with
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tail}
\ell_{\text{tail}} = \ell_{\text{head}}\,(n_{\max}-1) + 1 ,
\end{equation}
where $n_{\max}$ is the maximum function arity. Decoding walks the head
left-to-right in level order; each function token consumes its arity from the
next available slots in the head--tail stream, and the walk stops when the
expression tree closes. That prefix is the \emph{open reading frame} (ORF).
Everything past it is non-coding \emph{by definition}: the level-order decoder
never visits it, so its contents cannot affect the expressed tree.

Re-encoding exploits exactly this
(\texttt{src/karva.rs:406--437, 500--528}). A rewritten term is BFS-walked into a
\emph{natural head} containing every node of the tree, leaves included --- so the
natural head length equals the rewritten term's node count. If that is shorter
than the chromosome's configured $\ell_{\text{head}}$, it is extended to the
target with terminal filler. Those extra slots sit beyond the live
k-expression's BFS frontier, so the decoded tree, and hence the semantics, is
unchanged; it is the same syntactic position GEP fills with random tokens at
initialisation. The tail is then re-padded to Eq.~\ref{eq:tail} for the
\emph{final} head length. One subtlety is worth recording: $n_{\max}$ is the
\emph{primitive set's} maximum arity, not the maximum arity appearing in this
expression (\texttt{src/karva.rs:514--521}). A pure-unary term would otherwise get
a one-token tail, and a later point mutation to a binary operator --- which the
primitive set permits --- would run off the end of the stream.

Two routes implement the resize. \texttt{fuller} can do it itself, via the
optional \texttt{target\_head\_length} (\texttt{src/python.rs:250--253}), where an
over-long natural head is refused as \texttt{oversized} and never truncated. The
host engine instead resizes in the caller, immediately after
\texttt{denoise\_karva} returns
(\texttt{../hff/notebooks/\_gene\_utils.py:26--52}), and that code states the
invariant and its reason directly:

\begin{quote}\small
CRITICAL: the new gene MUST keep the ORIGINAL gene's fixed \texttt{head\_length}
\ldots{} a simplified karva often comes back shorter than the head it replaces. If
we sized the gene to \texttt{len(new\_head)} it would shrink below the fixed length
its siblings share, violating the GEP invariant and later crashing geppy's
length-dependent operators. Pad (or truncate) head and tail back to the declared
lengths so the rebuilt gene is a structural drop-in replacement of the same length.
\ldots{} Per GEP, the coding region (ORF) ends where the expression tree closes;
any head positions past the ORF are non-coding and ignored on expression, so
terminal padding is inert and cannot change the expressed tree.
\end{quote}

The re-injected chromosome is therefore a genuine same-length structural drop-in:
identical length, legal geometry, unchanged expressed tree. This step is sound
\emph{because} of the ORF, and would not be available in tree-GP.

\paragraph{The truncation branch [implemented, reachable, caught].} One caveat
deserves precision rather than hand-waving. The caller pads a short head but
\emph{truncates} a long one (\texttt{new\_head[:head\_length]}), and since the
live call omits \texttt{target\_head\_length}, \texttt{fuller} returns the
\emph{natural} head, whose length is the rewritten term's node count. So the
branch is reachable in principle: strict shrinkage does \emph{not} by itself
guarantee a fit, because a parent's ORF may extend into the tail, and an original
expressed tree can therefore exceed $\ell_{\text{head}}$ while a strictly smaller
rewrite still does. Truncation \emph{past} the ORF is inert, exactly like padding.
Truncation \emph{into} the ORF would change the expressed tree, and is the one
place in this design where semantics could move silently.

It does not escape. The rewritten term's nodes occupy the head's leading positions
by construction, so a cut at $\ell_{\text{head}}$ reaches coding material only
when node count exceeds $\ell_{\text{head}}$ --- and in that case the truncated
gene computes a different function, which the operator's own gate detects: it
recompiles the candidate through geppy's compiled callable, compares predictions
on a 64-row sample at relative tolerance $10^{-4}$, and on disagreement reverts to
the original individual and increments \texttt{rejected\_safety}
(\texttt{../hff/notebooks/\_denoise\_op.py:136--157, 238--243}). The failure mode
is a rejected mutation, not a corrupted population. The honest statement is that
in this configuration correctness rests on the caller's data gate rather than on
the representation alone; passing \texttt{target\_head\_length} would move the
guarantee back into the representation, refusing an over-long candidate outright.
Logging \texttt{rejected\_safety} against node count would settle whether the
branch is ever taken in practice.

\paragraph{Representation as an evolvable trait [implemented].} A constant can
appear in a genome two ways: as a symbol (\texttt{pi}, \texttt{G},
\texttt{k\_e}) or as a number ($3.14159\ldots$, $6.674\mathrm{e}{-}11$). The
\texttt{snap} $\rightleftharpoons$ \texttt{concretize} pair makes that choice a
reversible mutation (\texttt{src/snap\_karva.rs:149--193}). The up-flip proposes
lattice-constant substitutions, each \emph{verified in the e-graph} --- the
numeric literal and the symbolic form must land in the same e-class after
saturation --- rather than trusted on numeric closeness
(\texttt{src/snap\_karva.rs:265--271}); the down-flip replaces named constants by
their values and is behaviour-preserving by construction. A population therefore
carries both representations and selection decides which survives. When the
symbolic form wins, the discovered expression \emph{matches} the reference form
directly, instead of being recovered by post-hoc pattern matching.

\paragraph{Three operators, three soundness levels --- stated plainly.} It would
be easy to present \texttt{denoise}, \texttt{snap} and the physics-prior
mutations as uniformly ``sound by proof''. They are not, and the distinction
matters for anyone reading a result.
\emph{\texttt{denoise}} is exact identities plus a strict-shrink requirement plus
the $R^2$ gate.
\emph{\texttt{snap}} is \emph{not} an identity: lattice snaps hold only within a
significant-figure tolerance, which is why that ruleset is never loaded into
\texttt{denoise} or the parity scorer, where it would let the engine ``prove''
approximate equalities (\texttt{src/snap\_karva.rs:11--14}). It is e-graph-verified
within tolerance and then data-gated by the caller.
\emph{Physics-prior mutation} is a pure generator with no equivalence claim at
all; candidates it marks \texttt{speculative} \emph{change what the gene computes}
and must be gated on extrapolation rather than holdout
(\texttt{src/physics.rs:1--12, 27--30}). Only the first is behaviour-preserving in the
strong sense.

\paragraph{An honest observation [to be confirmed].} Editing genes led the
population in ways I found \emph{surprising} --- not necessarily wrong, but
unexpected. Convergence behaviour changed in character, not merely in rate. I do
not claim the mechanism is understood, and I do not claim the net effect is
positive: whether Lamarckian rewriting helps or hurts an SR run is
\textbf{unresolved}, and Section~\ref{sec:experiments} proposes the instrumentation
to settle it rather than an argument that it works.

\paragraph{Leading hypothesis: simplification is also a mutation source [to be
confirmed].} One mechanism is specific enough to test, and it follows from the
padding rather than from any defect in it. The filler terminals are chosen
\emph{at random} --- \texttt{rng.choice(terminals)} for both head and tail
(\texttt{../hff/notebooks/\_gene\_utils.py:46--52}), reseeded per simplification
event --- so every accepted edit injects \emph{fresh random non-coding material}
into the chromosome. That material is inert on expression, and cannot change what
the gene computes now. But it is new raw genetic material sitting in the
non-coding region, and crossover and transposition can later move it \emph{into}
a coding region, where it becomes expressed.

If this is the dominant effect, then \texttt{denoise} is not only a simplifier: it
is silently also a mutation operator, supplying variation at a rate proportional
to how often it fires and to how much it shrinks (a bigger reduction pads more
positions). That would explain a population moving in surprising-but-not-wrong
ways --- the expressed semantics of every individual edit are provably unchanged,
while the population's latent variation is being continuously refreshed. It also
predicts an interaction with the \texttt{pb\_each\_gene} gate, since the injection
rate scales with how many genes are visited per individual. This is a hypothesis,
not a finding; E3 proposes the instrumentation to test it directly.

\section{Proposed Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

This section is a named sketch. No numbers are claimed except where a run exists
in the repository and is cited as such.

\paragraph{E1. \texttt{fuller} versus SymPy in the SR loop [proposed].} The host
engine calls \texttt{fuller} through geppy/DEAP mutation operators
(\texttt{../hff/notebooks/\_denoise\_op.py}) with the configuration in
\texttt{hff\_sr\_engine.py}: per-individual denoise probability $0.20$, a
64-row safety re-check at tolerance $10^{-4}$, physics-prior mutation at $0.20$
with 8 candidates per gene, snap applied to offspring and the down-flip at
$0.05$. The operator re-evaluates a changed chromosome through geppy's own
compiled callable before swapping, and logs a \texttt{rejected\_safety} count
when predictions disagree. The experiment holds the GA fixed and swaps only the
simplifier, reporting wall-clock per generation, crash and timeout rate, final
expression size, and recovery rate of known laws on a Feynman-style benchmark.
Note that the host engine now registers raw operators \emph{decode-only} so that
\texttt{fuller}-emitted chromosomes stay compilable without entering random gene
generation; a SymPy arm must match that primitive set to be comparable.

\paragraph{E2. Rewriting as a genetic operator, on versus off [partially done,
for a different target].} Does Lamarckian simplification help? Report convergence
rate, final fitness, and population diversity, watching specifically for
diversity collapse --- the expected failure mode, since simplification is a
contraction toward canonical forms. Two confounds must be controlled, both from
Section~\ref{sec:operator}: the \texttt{pb\_each\_gene} gate sets how many genes
per individual are visited, and each accepted edit reseeds that gene's non-coding
padding, so an ``on'' arm differs from ``off'' in injected variation as well as in
simplification. Sweeping \texttt{pb\_each\_gene}, and running the fixed-terminal
padding variant of E3 as a third arm, separates the two effects.
A run of exactly this shape exists for Brainfuck rather than SR
(\texttt{examples/bf\_bloat/RESULTS.md}): population 30, 34 generations, 10 seeds,
300 individuals per arm, the simplified offspring replacing the original when
strictly shorter and not less fit. Solve rate was $41.0\%$ off versus $70.0\%$ on,
with mean length $10.9 \to 10.2$ and median $11 \to 9$. The caveats in that
document belong with the number: the task is a 3-operator ground truth, the
simplifier cannot invent the I/O operators needed to solve it, two of ten seeds
showed no benefit, and the soundness evidence is differential --- 2000 interpreter
comparisons with zero mismatches, but the fuzzer emits no brackets, so the
clear-loop rules are untested. It is encouraging evidence for the mechanism on an
exact target, and it is \emph{not} evidence for SR, where the gate is statistical.

\paragraph{E3. Lineage tracing of edited genes [proposed] --- the surprise.}
Instrument every operator application with: parent and child chromosome, which
operator fired, cost before and after, the $R^2$ loss, whether the safety re-check
rejected the swap, the rewritten term's node count against $\ell_{\text{head}}$
(to detect the truncation branch of Section~\ref{sec:operator}), and the edit's
generation. Then plot, per generation, the share of the population descended from
an edited gene; expressed-tree depth and node count distributions for edited
versus unedited lineages; and survival curves of edited genes against their
unedited siblings.

The decisive measurement for the mutation-source hypothesis is narrower: tag every
\emph{padded position} at the moment it is written, and count how often a tagged
position later enters an ORF --- via crossover, transposition, or a head mutation
that lengthens the coding region --- and what happens to the fitness of the
individual when it does. Report the per-generation count of padded positions
injected (which scales with edit frequency, shrinkage magnitude and
\texttt{pb\_each\_gene}) alongside inert-region diversity, measured as distinct
tail content across the population. The clean A/B is to replace
\texttt{rng.choice(terminals)} with a \emph{fixed} neutral terminal: if the
surprising behaviour is driven by injected variation it should attenuate, and if it
persists the mechanism lies elsewhere. That single-line change makes the hypothesis
falsifiable.

\paragraph{E4. Does carrying both representations help? [proposed]} Compare three
arms: numeric only; \texttt{snap} applied post-hoc to the final answer; and the
full \texttt{snap}/\texttt{concretize} pair active as mutations during the run.
Measure exact recovery of reference forms, not merely fit. The companion
paper~\cite{morgan2026tournaments} records the relevant trap --- a fitted
coefficient matched $\sqrt3$ to $1.5\times10^{-11}$ while $\sqrt3$ appeared
nowhere in the truth --- so a snap must be scored by whether the snapped law
reproduces held-out data, never by residual alone.

\paragraph{E5. Ruleset confluence [proposed, to justify a measured decision].}
The family split (Section~\ref{sec:design}) rests on observed blow-up. Quantify
it: for each family combination, saturate a fixed corpus under a fixed iteration
budget and record e-node count against iteration, plus time to the budget. This
converts a kill-guarded operational rule into a table. Context for the same
section: measured SymPy-parity across frozen corpora is roughly $33.6\%$ overall
(\texttt{powsimp} $84.9\%$, \texttt{trigsimp} $47\%$, \texttt{radsimp}/%
\texttt{ratsimp} $\approx15\%$, \texttt{simplify} $13\%$), as recorded in the
repository's \texttt{CLAUDE.md}; that file predates roughly the last twenty
commits, so the figures are to be re-measured. It is a deliberate
scope statement, and partly a measurement artifact --- SymPy rationalises to
\emph{larger} canonical forms than SR wants --- but \texttt{fuller} is a
replacement for SymPy \emph{in this loop}, not a general CAS, and the paper should
say so.

\paragraph{E6. HFF extraction versus stock scalar extraction [proposed].} Both
paths exist (Section~\ref{sec:extraction}), so hold the saturation budget and
candidate count fixed and compare selected-form quality: node count, transcendental
nesting, held-out error, and agreement with the reference form. The companion
paper's enumeration pitfall must be respected --- seed the candidate list with the
input form, or the tournament can rank a compact form behind thousands of
expanded variants.

\paragraph{E7. Ablation of the data gate [proposed].} How often does the $R^2$
tolerance reject a candidate the cost model preferred? Log every
\texttt{denoise} call with the cost-ranked winner and the accepted form. A near-zero
rejection rate would mean the gate is decoration and the identities suffice; a
high rate would mean the cost model alone is unsafe and would quantify the seam's
value directly. This is the cheapest experiment here and the most diagnostic.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Equality saturation and the e-graph as a rewriting substrate are due to
\texttt{egg}~\cite{egg} and its successor \texttt{egglog}~\cite{egglog}, which
adds Datalog-style rules and the named rulesets this work depends on.
Herbie~\cite{herbie} is the closest system in spirit --- same engine family, and
likewise selects among provably-equivalent forms by an explicit cost model ---
but its target is floating-point accuracy, and it is a standalone tool rather
than an operator inside a population. Gene expression programming and the karva
representation are due to Ferreira~\cite{ferreira2001}; the fixed head/tail
geometry and the ORF are properties of that representation, and
Section~\ref{sec:operator} argues GEP is the natural host for in-loop rewriting
for exactly that reason. SymPy~\cite{sympy} is the baseline. Simplification in GP
is conventionally post-processing, or an explicitly approximate ``numerical
simplification'' step; the contribution here is that proof of equivalence, or a
data gate, lets it move inside the loop. The hyperspherical projection and the
extraction tournament are developed in the companion
papers~\cite{morgan2026hff,morgan2026tournaments}.
