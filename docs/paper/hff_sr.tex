\title{Hyperspherical Fitness and Lineage Cohorts:\\
Recovering 75 of 133 SRBench Ground-Truth Laws\\
on One Seed at Six Minutes a Fit}

\author{HFF-SR}
\date{23 September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We report 75 of 133 ground-truth laws recovered exactly on the SRBench
ground-truth track at noise~0, using one development seed and 360~seconds per
problem. The whole run took 6\,h\,04\,m. AIFeynman, the best published entrant
on this track, recovers 54.1\% (72 of 133) using ten seeds at up to eight hours
a problem; the next best, GP-GOMEA, recovers 27.1\%. Our compute was 0.57\% of
the benchmark's allowance.

The change that produced the result is a selection restriction we call virtual
ALPS. Lineage instrumentation showed total diversity collapse: across four long
fits the pump drew 294{,}000 fresh individuals and not one of their descendants
survived. The cause was arithmetic, not competition --- a 105-draw tournament on
the measured configuration had a $6.7\times10^{-11}$ chance of containing no
converged elder, so young material was never sampled without one. We label each
row with the pump beat its line arrived on, and tournaments prefer candidates of
the same label. Strogatz recovery went from 3 of 14 to 7 of 14.

We also report what did not work: differentiated per-island rule sets lost,
the general mutation beam is exhausted at a 0.05\% hit rate, and combination
masking generated 30{,}180 candidates a beat and converted nothing. Two
limitations are stated plainly. This is one development seed, not the official
ten. And the p-value half of our stopping criterion is inert at this operating
point; the $R^2$ condition does all the work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

SRBench's ground-truth track asks whether a symbolic regression method recovers
the equation, not whether it fits the data. There are 133 problems: 119 from the
Feynman lectures and 14 from Strogatz's nonlinear dynamics text. A problem counts
as solved when SRBench's own scorer, \texttt{assess\_symbolic\_model}, judges the
submitted expression symbolically equivalent to the stored true model. A model
with test $R^2$ of $0.999999$ and the wrong shape scores zero.

This paper describes an engine built around four pieces: gene expression
programming with Karva K-expressions evaluated on the GPU; hyperspherical
fitness (HFF), which treats the objective vector as a point on a sphere and
ranks by its angle from the origin where every error vanishes; an island model
with a pump that refills an intake island from fresh random draws; and
\texttt{fuller}, an egglog-based rewriter that replaced sympy as the engine's
symbolic layer and converts back into the genotype. The result we report is 75
of 133.

The mechanism that got us there is one thing, not two. A search that converges
onto a single line cannot be improved by handing it a better expression: the
better expression is selected out before it can develop, which is the same
starvation Section~\ref{sec:alps} measures for fresh random individuals.
Cohort-restricted tournaments make space in the population for material that is
not yet competitive. Rewriting is what makes that space worth having, because it
is how a \emph{proposal} gets made --- a gene decodes to an expression, the
expression is rewritten, and the rewritten form converts back into a gene.
Neither half is the contribution on its own. A system that makes space for
edits, edits that are proposed rather than imposed, and selection that decides
which of them survives: that is the claim. Section~\ref{sec:together} states it
in full, and states plainly which part of it we have measured and which part we
have not.

Section~\ref{sec:system} describes the engine. Section~\ref{sec:alps} is the
main contribution: the diversity measurement, its arithmetic, the fix, and in
Section~\ref{sec:together} the interaction with rewriting and the experiment
that would isolate it. Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the run. Section~\ref{sec:failure} is the
failure-class analysis, which reframed what we work on next.
Section~\ref{sec:rewrites} covers data-guided rewrites, including one that
recovered a law never solved in any prior run, and one we declined to loosen.
Section~\ref{sec:negative} reports three negative results.
Section~\ref{sec:integrity} states the rules the run was held to, and
Section~\ref{sec:limits} the limitations.

\section{The system}
\label{sec:system}

\subsection{Genes on the device}

An individual is a gene expression programming chromosome: one to three genes,
each a Karva K-expression with a head of 34 symbols and a tail sized so that
every head arrangement decodes to a valid tree. Genes combine through a linker
--- average, product or sum --- and the linked result passes through an outer
wrapper, one of identity, $\log|x|$ or $\sqrt{|x|}$, so the model as scored is
$a \cdot W(L(g_0, g_1, g_2)) + b$ with $a$ and $b$ fitted by ordinary least
squares in the same pass that evaluates the tree. The chromosome carries its
best wrapper as it carries its best linker, and the wrapper travels with the row
through selection and through every edit. The whole population is a flat buffer of \texttt{u32}
tokens and \texttt{f32} constants; selection, mutation and crossover are four
compute kernels, one dispatch each per generation.

Two decisions here matter later. The first is the dynamic gene-subset choice: a
chromosome is scored under every non-empty subset of its genes, each subset under
each linker, so 15 gene-linker combinations for three genes --- 45 candidates
once each is tried under each wrapper --- and it keeps the
best. A law that is one nested structure no longer needs the other two genes to
evolve into exact do-nothing values. On Feynman I.26.2 the engine ran 328
generations without the subset choice and did not solve it; with it, the fit
early-stopped at generation 14 using a single gene. It costs 2.7 times as much
per generation.

The second is that the engine runs in \texttt{f32} on the device. Compensated
summation was measured dead on this hardware --- the Metal compiler removed the
Neumaier correction and the device matched neither the compensated nor the plain
CPU twin. After that was fixed, along with a 1-ulp correction to the device's
division and square root, the \texttt{f32} floor on a true law fell from
$1.2\times10^{-10}$ to $1.1\times10^{-14}$. The stopping bar sits at
$10^{-10}$, so this was not a cosmetic fix.

\subsection{Hyperspherical fitness}
\label{sec:hff}

The engine computes up to nine error objectives: mean squared error, $1 - R^2$
and mean absolute error, each on three blocks of rows (train, validation, and a
third block that may be synthetic-noisy or edge rows). Each is capped at the
constant model's error on that block --- a model worse than predicting the mean
is useless, and how much worse carries no information --- and scaled into
$[0,1]$ against frozen ranges.

\textbf{The run reported here used six.} The third block is dropped entirely when
no synthetic or edge rows are generated, and the run generated none, so the
objective set was mean squared error, $1 - R^2$ and mean absolute error on train
and validation. The engine's two non-error objectives --- the tower described
below, and a redundancy column, a leave-one-gene-out score that charges a
chromosome for carrying genes whose removal costs the fit nothing --- were both
off. We state this from the run's recorded
settings and the engine's defaults: the run emitted no card recording the
derived objective count, and the card format that does postdates it.

The third block is where the two halves of the stop bar disagree, and we record
it as an open design question rather than a resolved one. When the block holds
synthetic noisy rows it exists to rank individuals in tournaments and is not
meant to decide that a fit is exact --- the $1 - R^2$ condition of
Section~\ref{sec:stopbar} honours that and skips it, but the angle does not, so
the block's error enters $p$ like any other objective. One of the two is wrong.
It did not bite in this run, which had no third block at all, but it bears on
the p-value discussion of Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

HFF then treats the scaled vector as a point on the unit sphere and returns its
angle from the pole where all errors are zero:
\[
\theta = \arccos\!\left(1 - \min\!\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_i x_i^2,\ 1\right)\right),
\]
with $m$ the number of objectives. Lower is fitter. There is no weighting, no
scalarisation of the objectives into one number by hand, and no parsimony term
in the fitness. Size is a reporting decision, taken after the search ends.

The pole matters and was chosen by measurement. HFF also admits a
\emph{balanced} pole --- the angle from $(1/\sqrt m, \ldots, 1/\sqrt m)$, which
rewards an individual for being even across its objectives rather than for being
close to zero on all of them. Ranking tournaments from that pole scored 26 laws
against 53 for the zero pole on the same seed with everything else equal, and
the zero pole recovered 30 laws the balanced pole missed. That is a larger swing
than any other single decision in this paper. The balanced pole remains
available for selection and is off.

Objectives can be entered on a log scale, $1 + \log_{10}(x)/12$, and the argument
for it is the accuracy we need. Squared as it stands, small errors weigh nothing: an
error of $5\times10^{-3}$ contributes $3\times10^{-5}$ and one of
$2\times10^{-4}$ contributes $4\times10^{-8}$. A tournament cannot tell a
plausible fake at $10^{-3}$ from an exact law at $10^{-12}$. On the log scale
those two sit at 0.75 and 0, and every factor of ten in the error counts the
same. The log scale is off by default and was off in this run; the argument
above is why it exists, not a measured contribution to 75.

The angle converts to a p-value: the probability that a point drawn uniformly on
the $m$-objective hypersphere lies within $\theta$ of the pole, the regularised
incomplete beta $I_{\sin^2\theta}\!\left(\frac{m-1}{2}, \frac12\right)$. We
report $\log_{10} p$ throughout, computed in log space so it stays finite deep
in the left tail where $p$ itself underflows.

One available objective is not an error. The \emph{tower} objective is the
transcendental
nesting depth of the decoded gene --- the most transcendental functions met on
any path from root to leaf. Addition, subtraction, multiplication, division,
negation, reciprocal and whole powers do not count, so a long flat law scores 0.
It is off by default, and off in the run of Section~\ref{sec:results}; it was on
in the lineage measurement of Section~\ref{sec:measurement}. We describe it
because it is a component of the engine, not because it contributed to 75.
This is not parsimony. It was calibrated on 1{,}230 labelled fits: every one of
SRBench's 133 true laws has a depth of at most 2, and three quarters of the
accurate-but-wrong models are 3 or more. The penalty has a free zone up to depth
2 because charging from depth 0 makes the search settle for flat polynomials ---
it rebuilt $mc^2 + mv^2/2$ in place of $mc^2/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ and never tried
a root.

\subsection{The pump and the islands}

The population is organised in pairs. Each pair has an intake island and a
champion island. On the pump's beat, the intake's best two individuals are copied
over the champion island's worst two; the intake then keeps its best fifth ---
one of each distinct row --- and the rest is refilled with fresh random
individuals. A cross step, off by default, lets each intake island take in the
best of the other pairs' champion islands.

The design intent is plain: the champion island accumulates, the intake island
churns, and the pump is where diversity enters. Section~\ref{sec:alps} is about
the measurement that showed it was not entering.

\subsection{\texttt{fuller}: rewriting that converts back into the genotype}
\label{sec:rewriting}

\texttt{fuller} is a Rust crate built on egglog 2.0 that replaced sympy in the
engine. It holds a real-domain expression datatype with the engine's protected
operators as distinct functions from the raw ones, a real-domain evaluator whose
semantics match the device exactly, and several rulesets: algebraic identities,
powers, distribution, rational arithmetic and trigonometry.

The important structural point is that \texttt{fuller} converts in both
directions. A chromosome decodes to an expression, the expression is rewritten,
and the rewritten form converts \emph{back into a Karva gene}. That return leg
is what makes a rewrite available to the search rather than only to the report,
and it is the capability the argument of Section~\ref{sec:together} rests on.

Two rewriters use it, and only one of them ran inside the search that produced
75. \emph{Constant snapping} is the one that did: a folded literal near $\pi$,
$e$, $\sqrt2$ or a physical constant is matched against a resident lattice on
the GPU, judged on whether the whole model's $R^2$ survives, and written back
into the gene in place. \emph{Saturate-and-extract} --- decode, run the
e-graph to a bounded fixpoint, enumerate variants, score them on the data and
keep the smallest within tolerance --- is the algebraic half. It is implemented
and exposed to callers over the crate's Python interface, and it has no call
site in the engine's generation loop; it was a stated design for the search, not
a component of this run. Section~\ref{sec:together} is exact about which was
which and why the distinction matters to the claim.

Two things about this substrate cost us time. Rule
families are non-confluent: distribution co-saturated with trigonometry or with
rational arithmetic explodes the e-graph and pegs a CPU core. The
saturate-and-extract path therefore uses only a bounded algebra-plus-powers
subset, and
distribution is kept out of it entirely --- it is a normal-form canonicaliser,
and enumerating variants over its equivalence class does not terminate in useful
time. And egglog does not constant-fold floating-point literals unless a rule
says to, which is a correctness feature and a repeated surprise.

Against frozen sympy corpora, generated offline so that sympy is never in the
scoring loop, \texttt{fuller} reaches about 33.6\% parity overall: 84.9\% on
\texttt{powsimp}, 47\% on \texttt{trigsimp}, around 15\% on \texttt{radsimp}
and \texttt{ratsimp}, and 13\% on \texttt{simplify}. The low radical and rational
figures are partly a measurement artifact --- sympy rationalises to larger
canonical forms, not the smaller expression a symbolic regressor wants --- but
they are not solved.

\subsection{The stopping bar}
\label{sec:stopbar}

A fit stops when \emph{both} train and validation $1 - R^2 \le 10^{-10}$ --- and
the third block's too, when a third block exists and it is edge rows rather than
synthetic ones --- and $\log_{10} p \le -19$. The run of
Section~\ref{sec:results} had no third block, so its bar was train, validation
and $p$.

The p-value half was added after a measurement on development seed 7013: every
real law that met the $R^2$ condition sat at $-19.4$ or below, most between $-21$
and $-22$, and the one fake that slipped under the $R^2$ bar (Strogatz
\texttt{bacres2}, validation $1-R^2 = 1.0\times10^{-11}$) sat at $-17.55$.

The train half was added later and for the same failure from the other side. A
validation split can be lucky: over the cascade's 117 early stops, all 113 real
wins had train $1-R^2 \le 10^{-10}$ with a median of $9.8\times10^{-15}$, and
the single exception was \texttt{bacres2} again --- stopping with validation ten
times better than the rows the model was actually fitted on, which is a
description of noise rather than of a fit. Requiring both sides cost no true win
on record. It was in place before the run reported here. Note what this means
for the p-value's job: \texttt{bacres2} is the law both halves of the bar were
hardened against, and the later of its two false stops is caught on the error
side without $p$ being consulted. Section~\ref{sec:limits} returns to whether
$p$ is still earning its place.

The bar is ours. It never reads SRBench's verdict. That distinction does real
work in Section~\ref{sec:integrity}.

\section{Virtual ALPS: couples from the same century}
\label{sec:alps}

\subsection{The measurement}
\label{sec:measurement}

We instrumented every individual with an identity, an age (generations since its
genotype entered the population, by Hornby's ALPS rule: an offspring is its
parent's age plus one, a fresh random individual is 0) and a lineage back to the
founder its line began at. The log is written only when a path is given; with it
unset the engine is bit-identical to before and costs nothing. Nothing about
selection changed for this measurement, and no age layers were added.

Six fits on seed 7013, population 1{,}500 intake plus 1{,}500 champion, head 34,
30~seconds each, with the tower objective on, no validation block, a constant
range of $\pm5$ and the pump on every fifth generation, on a GPU shared with
other running fits so the timings are under contention.
Table~\ref{tab:lineage} is the result.

Two readings, both stark.

Every winner's founder was drawn in the initial population. Not one fit was won
by a line the pump introduced later. That is consistent with an earlier finding
that 54\% of solves are already present in the initial population and 69\% by
generation 5.

The population converges onto one line and the pump never breaks it. Across the
four long fits the pump drew 294{,}000 fresh individuals --- 40 to 72 refills of
roughly 1{,}200 rows each. Of the 73{,}990 rows later kept as the intake's best
fifth or promoted into a champion island, the founder origin was \texttt{init}
for every single one. Zero came from a pump refill. A fresh draw's descendants
never once reached the top fifth of the island they were drawn into.

The founder count makes the timescale exact. \emph{Founders, all rows} reads
1201 on precisely the two fits that ended \emph{on} a pump beat ($335 = 67
\times 5$, $360 = 72 \times 5$): that is the 1{,}200 rows just drawn, plus one
line for the other 1{,}800. On the two fits that ended off the beat it reads 1.
Every one of 1{,}200 fresh lines is extinct across all 3{,}000 rows within two
generations of arriving.

\subsection{The arithmetic}

This is not a competition result. It is a sampling result, and it is arithmetic.

A refilled intake holds 300 keepers --- its best fifth, by construction fitter
than anything just drawn --- among 1{,}500 rows. The tournament size is 7\% of
1{,}500, which is 105. The probability that a single 105-draw tournament contains
no keeper at all is
\[
\left(\frac{1200}{1500}\right)^{105} = 6.7\times10^{-11},
\]
and selection is two-stage, so the real figure is tighter still. Young material
is not outcompeted. It is never \emph{sampled} without a converged elder in the
same draw. No amount of fresh genetic material fixes this, because the fresh
material was never the bottleneck.

This is why we are willing to carry the measurement across configurations. The
six instrumented fits did not use the objective set or the constant range of the
run in Section~\ref{sec:results} --- no validation block, the tower on, a
narrower constant range that leaves constant snapping with little to bite on.
The arithmetic above is the reason that does not matter here: it is a statement
about tournament size against keeper fraction, and it contains no term for the
objectives, for what is being fitted, or for how the winner is scored. Any
configuration that refills a fifth of an island and draws 105-candidate
tournaments from it starves young material at the same rate.

\subsection{The change}

Hornby's ALPS solves this with physical age layers: the population is split into
bands by age, an individual too old for its band is moved up or discarded, and
the top layer is unbounded. We kept the selection restriction and dropped the
physical layers.

Each row carries a cohort label: the pump beat its line arrived on, inherited by
every descendant. It is a \emph{lineage} cohort, not an age --- a fresh mutation
of an elder is still an elder, which is exactly what keeps young material from
meeting one in a tournament. A tournament run by a row of cohort $c$ prefers a
candidate of cohort $c$ over any other, however fit the other is.

The device implementation is branchless. Rather than reject and redraw, which
would put a loop on the GPU, the cohort is the first sort key and fitness the
second. This is the comparator as it stood for the run reported here, at commit
\texttt{05ef7e9}; it has since gained a per-island flag that can lift the
restriction, and the run applied it on both islands of the pair:

\begin{verbatim}
fn better_mate(me: u32, c: u32, w: u32) -> bool {
    let mc = same_cohort(me, c);
    let mw = same_cohort(me, w);
    if (mc != mw) { return mc; }
    return key(c) < key(w);
}
\end{verbatim}

A mismatched candidate simply loses. When a tournament contains no match at all,
the best of the draw still wins, so a cohort that has died out costs nothing and
needs no special case. The whole mechanism is one \texttt{u32} per row and no
extra dispatch.

\texttt{cohort\_merge} is the threshold past which cohorts stop being separate:
cohorts at or beyond it are clamped to one band, so the elders co-mingle freely
and only the young are kept apart. That is Hornby's unbounded top layer,
expressed as a clamp. In the run reported here it was a clamp on the label
itself (\texttt{05ef7e9}); the engine now clamps on age, \texttt{generation -
label}, which is the intended reading and was a bug fix --- banding on the label
alone holds the oldest lines apart permanently and merges the youngest, the
opposite of the design.

\textbf{The merge was effectively never reached in the run either way.} It was
set to 10{,}000 against fits of a few thousand generations --- at the rates of
Section~\ref{sec:results}, a Feynman fit reaches about 4{,}300 generations in its
360~seconds and only the longest Strogatz fits cross the threshold at all. So the
75 was produced with the cohorts permanently separate and the unbounded top layer
inactive. The elder merge is a design we describe and have not tested; whether
the result depends on its absence is an open question, and a cheaper experiment
than the one Section~\ref{sec:together} asks for.

Setting \texttt{cohort\_merge} to 0 turns cohorts off entirely, and the CPU/GPU
bit-for-bit parity tests then prove the engine is what it always was. It must
also exceed the pump period to mean anything: a merge that fires inside a single
beat is the mechanism switched off wearing labels, which the engine asserts
against.

\subsection{What it measured}
\label{sec:measured}

On one law --- Feynman III.8.54, the hardest we had --- the change is
unambiguous. Measured over a single 500-generation fit at population 10{,}000
intake plus 5{,}000 champion, same seed both arms: surviving lines went from 1
to 483, the fraction of survivors whose founder came from the pump went from
0\% to 100\%, and $1 - R^2$ went from $6.70\times10^{-1}$ to
$1.12\times10^{-1}$. That is one law and one fit, not a sweep.

Strogatz \texttt{barmag2} is the clean proof. It had been stuck at $1 - R^2 =
6.9\times10^{-3}$ in a converged monoculture. With cohorts on it early-stops at
generation 614 with $1 - R^2 = 6.66\times10^{-14}$.

Across the benchmark, Strogatz recovery went from 3 of 14 to 7 of 14. That is
the clearest attribution in the run, because the cohorts were built for exactly
this failure and Strogatz is where it bit hardest.

\subsection{Space for editing, and what we have not measured}
\label{sec:together}

Cohorts and rewriting are presented above as two pieces of engineering. We think
they are one mechanism, and the case for it is short.

The unusual capability is the round trip of Section~\ref{sec:rewriting}: a gene
decodes to an expression, the expression is rewritten into an equivalent form,
and that form converts \emph{back into a gene} the search can breed from. Most
symbolic regression systems convert one way only --- a genotype becomes an
expression, the expression becomes a number, and the number is where it ends.
Converting back is what makes a rewrite available to the search at all, rather
than to the report.

It is worth being exact about which rewriter did that in the run reported here.
The operator that writes into a live gene is the constant snap of
Section~\ref{sec:rewriting}: a folded constant is matched against the resident
lattice, the substitution is kept only if the whole model's $R^2$ survives it,
and the accepted form is written back into the gene in place. That is a repair
of an individual rather than a proposal beside it --- the row keeps its identity,
its age, its line and its cohort, and the guard, not a tournament, is what
accepted the form. The algebraic half of \texttt{fuller}, the saturate-and-extract
round trip, is a capability the crate provides and a stated design for the
engine; it is not a component of the run that scored 75.

The design the argument calls for is the stronger one: an edited form entering
the population \emph{beside} the original rather than over it, unevaluated, with
the tournament as the only thing that judges it. Then nothing is imposed. The
engine would have no way to declare a rewritten form better and would not try
to; it would enter the pool as a candidate and be held to the same fitness as
every other row. That is guidance through equivalence rather than steering by
hand.

Since the run we have built one operator in that shape, and report it here
because its result so far is not a good one. A constant-folding edit --- a
subtree that flattens its input to a constant is collapsed to that constant ---
lands the cleaned gene in its island's worst row, unevaluated, leaving the
original untouched for the tournament to choose between them. Across three seeds
at 400 generations it is one win and two losses, and we claim nothing from it:
400 generations is a poor test of an operator aimed at a constant blob carried
for sixty thousand, so what the A/B establishes is that the mechanism runs, not
that it pays. It also exposes a tension the argument above does not
resolve: the proposal inherits its parent's cohort rather than a fresh one, so
it fights in its parent's band and does \emph{not} get the protected young band
that this section argues a proposal needs. Which of those two a proposal should
wear is the open question, and it is not the same question as the missing row
below.

That is exactly the material Section~\ref{sec:alps} shows a converged population
destroys. A proposal is, at the moment it is made, a row whose structure is
untried. Dropped into a monoculture it would be drawn into a 105-candidate
tournament against a converged elder with probability $1 - 6.7\times10^{-11}$,
and it would be gone, however good the form it carries. Cohort restriction is
what would give it a protected band to develop in before it has to answer to an
incumbent. The two halves need each other in both directions: an edit without
protection does not survive
to be judged on its merits, and protection without edits is age-layered
selection over random variation, which is worth having only when there is
something worth protecting.

\textbf{We have not isolated this.} The 75-of-133 run had both cohorts and snap
on, and so did the cascade it is compared against. What the record supports is
an attribution of the cohort change \emph{given} editing on: the same-seed arm
on Feynman III.8.54 in Section~\ref{sec:measured}, the cascade at 66 against
this run's 75, and Strogatz 3 of 14 to 7 of 14. What the record does not contain
is a single fit with editing switched off, in either cohort condition. The
two-by-two has one row, not one cell, and the missing row is the
one that would show whether the cohort gain depends on there being proposals to
protect or would have arrived just as well over random variation alone. The
negative result in Section~\ref{sec:beam} does not fill the gap: it measures a
generated mutant against its own original at the moment it is made, which is a
different question from whether a protected band lets a proposal develop into a
solve. The experiment is cheap and we have not run it.

\section{The run}
\label{sec:results}

One development seed (7014), 360~seconds per fit, 133 problems, 6\,h\,04\,m
total. The settings: 800 intake plus 400 champion, pump every 100 generations,
\texttt{cohort\_merge}~$=10{,}000$, gene subsets on, generation cap 50{,}000 so
that time binds everywhere. The generation cap matters --- Strogatz problems run
at about 33 generations per second against Feynman's 12, and a Feynman-sized
generation cap was cutting Strogatz fits off with a third of their time budget

\subsection{Sequencing}

The run was ordered easy-first by our own signal: how fast the stop bar fired for

ever solved was banked before the hard half began.

The sequencing work also exposed a harness bug: the
\texttt{--problems} ordering was being applied to an already-shuffled list, so
the order asked for was discarded. It affected which problems ran when, not which
of them solved.

\subsection{The double submission}

Each fit submits two strings: \texttt{fuller}'s own final form, and a

are reporting wins rather than search wins.

\section{Why the unsolved laws failed}
\label{sec:failure}

This analysis is of the \emph{cascade} run, the 66-solve predecessor on the same
development seed, not of the 75-solve run reported above. The cascade left 67
laws unsolved. We classified every one of them by why, not by how close it came.
The exercise reframed the work more than any single mechanism did.

B. No model scored & SRBench's scorer returned \texttt{None} --- our string is too long to simplify & 16 \\
C. Hit the generation cap & the cap, not the search, ended it (7 of them Strogatz) & 11 \\

Class D dominates: 53 of 67 are the search simply not reaching the law within
the time, and no amount of work on the reporting path touches them. But class B
is the largest \emph{recoverable} class, and it is entirely ours to fix --- the
scorer is not refusing our answer, it is failing to parse a 300-character string
in the time it allows. Class C is the cheapest fix of all, and it fed directly
into the generation-cap change described in Section~\ref{sec:results}: seven of
those eleven are Strogatz problems that were cut off with a third of their time
budget unspent.

Three diagnostics came out of the analysis, and each is a check we can run on our
own output before submitting anything.

\subsection{Rounding collapse}

SRBench's scorer rounds every float in the submitted expression to three decimals
(and anything below $10^{-4}$ to zero) before comparing. A model that is the law
to eleven decimals can collapse to nothing under that transform.

The worked case is Strogatz \texttt{lv2}. The engine reported
\[
-4096 \cdot y \cdot \tan(\tan(0.000244\,(x + y - 2))).
\]
Since $\tan(\tan(\epsilon u)) \approx \epsilon u$ for small $\epsilon u$, and
$4096 \times 0.000244 \approx 1$, this expression \emph{is}
$-y(x+y-2)$, which is the law. It passed both halves of our stop bar. Then the
scorer rounded $0.000244$ to zero, the entire term collapsed, and the submitted
model was the constant $0$.

This is knowable before we submit, and cheaply. Round every float to three
decimals exactly as \texttt{symbolic\_utils.py::round\_floats} does; if the
result \texttt{is\_number}, the form has lost all its structure and can never
match a law whatever its $R^2$. A single \texttt{xreplace} over the float atoms
does this in 0.2~ms, against 9.9~ms for SRBench's per-float \texttt{subs} loop
--- same answer, 50 times faster, cheap enough to run inside every fit.

This is not reading the benchmark's verdict. It is a published, deterministic
transform applied to our own string, in the same class as the existing guard
that asserts the reported string computes what the chromosome computes. The
final-form selection now sorts on (survives rounding, size, spelling), so a
surviving form beats a smaller dying one and among equals the smallest still
wins.

\subsection{Non-finite coverage}

We evaluated the reported plain-form model of all 133 laws on the test rows.

Every single solve is on the finite side. Not one of the 38 was ever solved, in
any run. In 133 laws it is a perfect predictor with no exceptions.

The cause is the protected/plain split. The engine scores the \emph{faithful}
form, where a protected logarithm returns a guard value on a negative argument.
What we submit is the \emph{plain} form, where the same expression is a raw
logarithm and returns NaN on those rows. The two agree wherever the guard never
fires and diverge everywhere else. Feynman II.11.28 scores $1 - R^2 =
8\times10^{-6}$ on the faithful form while the string we hand the benchmark is
undefined on 59\% of the data. Four laws are finite on 0\% of rows.

This is also knowable before submitting: evaluate our own reported string on
rows we already hold. We first reported 16 laws as non-finite, from a script
that skipped a law on timeout and then re-read a different stage's fit for it.
Re-run with one record per law, the count is 38.

\subsection{Bloat}

Over all 342 fits of the cascade, a fit whose \texttt{simplified\_symbolic\_model}
came back \texttt{None} had a median model of 323 characters. One that scored had
122. The scorer is not refusing our answer; it is failing to parse it within the
time it allows. A numerically excellent model 300 characters long scores zero.

These three classes overlap rather than partition. A bloated form is also the
kind that reaches a protected operator, and both are the kind that survives
rounding only by accident.

\section{Data-guided rewrites}
\label{sec:rewrites}

The engine's protected operators are honest about their domains and useless to a
symbolic scorer. A \emph{data-guided rewrite} is a rewrite the final form may
make because the data says it holds on every row --- never an identity in
general, always exact on the rows the model was selected on, and always checked:
the rewritten model must predict what the original predicts, to the same
tolerance the rest of the rewriter is held to.

The catalogue is long and dull individually. \texttt{Abs}$(e)$ where $e$ keeps
one sign on every row becomes $e$ or $-e$. A protected division whose denominator
is at least $10^{-6}$ on every row becomes real division. A protected arcsine
whose argument stays inside $[-1,1]$ becomes the raw arcsine. $\log$ of a product
with an $\exp(u)$ factor, the rest of it positive on every row, becomes $u + \log
c$ --- sympy will not cancel $\log(\exp u)$ without knowing $u$ is real, and
Feynman II.10.9 was the law behind $0.2\log(\exp(5u)/5) + 0.3218\ldots$ and was
scored as wrong. A factor standing on both sides of a product's line, never zero
on the data, cancels; that one is what finally let Feynman II.13.23's
$\tan(\arcsin(v/c))/v$ become the law, since it is only there once the $v$ and
the $c$ go.

Collectively the rewrites recovered ten laws the engine had already \emph{found}
and the scorer had rejected: II.11.27, III.12.43, I.18.12, I.14.4, I.30.5,
III.17.37, Strogatz \texttt{glider2}, Strogatz \texttt{barmag2}, II.10.9 and
I.44.4.

\subsection{The worked case: \texttt{III\_17\_37}}

Feynman III.17.37 was found as
\[
-1.41421\,\sqrt{(144 - u)^2 / 2} + 144,
\qquad u = \beta\,(\alpha\cos\theta + 1).
\]
Since the fitted scale is $\sqrt2$ to nine digits, the model \emph{is} $144 - |144 - u|$,
and $u < 144$ on every row, so it is $u$ --- the law, exactly.

The existing $\sqrt{e^2} \to |e|$ rule could not see it. The radicand is a
quotient, not a bare square, so the root stayed and no scorer matched the form.
Splitting a positive literal out of the radicand lets the fitted scale cancel
against it, and the absolute value then sheds the sign the data settles.

Measured on seed 7014, III.17.37 goes from $\log_{10} p = -30.61$ scored as
nothing, to solved on both submission arms at generation 39, as
$x_0 x_1 \cos(x_2) + x_0$. No run on record had ever recovered it.

\subsection{The rule we declined to loosen}

$\tan(u)$, $\sin(u)$, $\tanh(u)$ and $\arcsin(u)$ are all $u$ once $|u|$ is small
enough. We added the small-angle rewrite, data-guided like the rest, bounded at
$|u| < 10^{-5}$ on every row. At that bound the cubic term ($u^3/3$ for $\tan$)
is under $3.4\times10^{-16}$ relative, which is inside f64's own noise.

Strogatz \texttt{lv2} --- the rounding-collapse case above --- would be captured
by this rule if the bound were loosened, and that is one more law. Its argument
reaches $1.95\times10^{-3}$. At that magnitude the substitution carries a
$1.3\times10^{-6}$ relative error: ten orders of magnitude above f64 epsilon, and
large enough to move the predictions.

We declined. Loosening the bound to catch \texttt{lv2} would be asserting an
identity that the data says is false. The bound stays at $10^{-5}$ and
\texttt{lv2} stays unsolved by this route.

\section{Negative results}
\label{sec:negative}

\subsection{Swim lanes lost}

The fourteen variation rates were one global set. We moved them onto the island
record, which the device already uploads as a flat table every generation, so a
per-island rule set costs rows and never a second dispatch. Each island pair then
breeds under its own \emph{lane}, written as what it changes relative to the
engine's own schedule: a multiplier on the point-mutation and transposition
rates, a multiplier on the three crossover rates, and a cleansing rate. With the
cross step off, lanes never mix.

The guarantee came first: a three-lane table of the engine's own rules produces
an identical model, identical unique genes and identical individuals to no lane
table at all. The engine is deterministic on a seed, so that assertion is exact.

Differentiated lanes then lost. Over ten problems on seed 7013, the uniform arm
--- three lanes all carrying the engine's own schedule --- solved 4; the
differentiated arm, running an explorer lane and a refiner lane beside the
general one, solved 3. The differentiated arm also lost Feynman I.15.10, a
Lorentz law the uniform arm solved at generation 186 while the differentiated
arm ran 958 generations without it.

The mechanism is sound and cheap, and the hypothesis that three rule sets
running in parallel beat one was not supported. A law lost outright is a worse
signal than a one-problem count difference is a good one.

\subsection{The general mutation beam is exhausted}
\label{sec:beam}

The beam takes the best individual as it stands and generates thousands of
mutations of it, scored on the data, with the original never lost. The tree half
of the beam is the cleanse neighbourhood enumerated whole --- every function node
of every used gene, promoted over each child and collapsed to a constant --- plus
drawn point, Dc and constant edits.

Over six near misses, a beam mutant beat its original 0.05\% of the time.
For comparison, changing the population size alone moved $\log_{10} p$ by up to
1.8 decades on the same problems --- more than the beam ever did. The tree half
is now off by default; the beat is the functional wraps alone, and the tree half
survives as a knob its tests turn on.

The wrap half of the beam has its own negative result, which is instructive
because it was a representational failure rather than a search failure. Wraps
were originally scored on the host gene alone, to make score and graft agree by
construction. That formulation cannot express four of the five laws it was aimed
at: I.10.7 is $m_0/\sqrt{1 - (v/c)^2}$, I.48.2 is $mc^2/\sqrt{\cdots}$, I.34.1 is
$\omega_0/(1 - v/c)$, III.4.33 is $k_B T \cdot u/(e^u - 1)$. All four are
$\text{prefactor}(\text{variables}) \times W(u)$, and with only a scalar outside
the wrap the prefactor has nowhere to live. Measured over 24 fits on two seeds:
8{,}300 wrap candidates, 0 better, 0 grafted, 0 kept.

Moving the wrap inside the linker fixed the representation. It also exposed a
different bug, because a reader should not assume score and graft
agree for free: on a three-gene model, the wrap $1/(x-1)$ scored $1-R^2 =
0.0759$ and its graft computed $1.0100$. A wrap that appeared to explain 92\% of
the variance landed as worse than the mean, because it was scored on
$W(L(g_0,g_1,g_2))$ and grafted as $W(g_0)$ with the other genes set to 1.

\subsection{Combination masking converted nothing}

The cleanse neighbourhood is a diagonal mask: one node removed at a time. It
cannot reach a nest where no single node is removable but several together are,
which is the shape of the bloated near misses --- Feynman I.10.7 is a
23-character law wearing 20 function calls, and dropping any single one of them
loses the fit.

So we built the combination mask: draw $k \ge 2$ nodes at once, enumerate pairs
while they fit the beam's width and draw randomly past that, each step re-reading
the gene so the pick is over what is left. The output is an ordinary gene, so the
existing device path scores it with everything else.

Measured on one law and one fit (Feynman II.24.17), it generated 30{,}180
mutants a beat where the wrap-only design made 180. Eleven of them beat their
original and three landed. Nothing converted into a solve.

The reason is structural and we should have seen it earlier: masking can only
\emph{remove} structure. A law that needs a term the search never built is
unreachable by any amount of deletion, at any $k$.

\subsection{Brute force does not scale the way we expected}

A single timing breakdown puts 9.06~s of a 15.6~s fit in data evaluation ---
58\% --- rather than in population handling. Larger populations therefore buy
much less than the individuals-per-second figure suggests. A directly measured instance:
a population of 20{,}000 with a 35-generation cap scored 42 of 133, against 46
for the ordinary configuration. That arm also carried a leave-one-gene-out
redundancy objective, so it is not a clean population-only test; what it does
show is that the combination gained four problems the smaller run lacked and
lost more than it gained.

\section{Benchmark integrity}
\label{sec:integrity}

The rules this run was held to, stated so they can be checked.

\textbf{No restarts.} One fit per problem, one search per fit. An earlier
experiment that split the time budget into three independent searches scored 54
of 133 and was rejected as cheating-like --- splitting the budget across
independent draws is what the ten official seeds are for, and taking the best of
three is not a single-seed score.

\textbf{Never generate data from a known formula.} The engine sees only the data
SRBench provides.

\textbf{The benchmark's verdict never steers the search.} Every stopping decision
and every sequencing decision uses our own criterion --- the $1-R^2$ and p-value
bar of Section~\ref{sec:system}. The prescore check of
Section~\ref{sec:failure} is a deterministic published transform applied to our
own string, not a query to the scorer.

\textbf{Development seeds only.} SRBench's official ten seeds are the test set.
Every number in this paper is from seeds 7001--7020.

\textbf{Name-blind columns.} Variables are presented as $x_0, x_1, \ldots$ so no
physical constant can be suggested by a variable name.

One defect in the benchmark costs us a law we cannot
recover by any means. SRBench's stored true model for Feynman II.6.15b is already
rounded to $0.239\, p_d \sin\theta\cos\theta / (\epsilon r^3)$. Its scorer
simplifies to the double angle, which halves the constant to $0.1194$, and then
rounds to three decimals: the truth becomes $0.12$ and our exact $3/(4\pi)$
becomes $0.119$. We ran the real scorer on four forms --- our fit, full precision,
symbolic $3/(4\pi)$, and the double-angle form --- and all four fail with the
identical residual. There is no form we can submit that passes.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{One seed.} This is one development seed, not the official ten. Same-seed
arms in our own experiments differ by about $\pm3$ problems from the draw alone,
and two seeds at identical settings have differed by 8 laws. The number would
move in either direction on a full ten-seed evaluation, and the comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:field} is not like-for-like on that axis. We state 75 as a
single-run development measurement, not as a rate.

We also keep it distinct from union figures. Across all configurations and seeds,
the engine has solved 77 of 133 distinct laws at least once, and an analysis of
laws that pass our stop bar but score as misses puts a ceiling from that line of
work at 82. Neither is a single-configuration score and neither is claimed as
one.

\textbf{The p-value is inert here.} Every $\log_{10} p$ in this run sits between
$-30$ and $-38$, against a bar of $-19$, and the solved and unsolved distributions
overlap completely, with medians of $-36.3$ and $-35.1$. The p-value never binds
and cannot separate a law from a near miss at this operating point; $1 - R^2$ is
doing all the work of the stop bar.

The reason is the angle, not the dimension. A $\log_{10} p$ bar is
dimension-free: $m$ enters the incomplete beta of Section~\ref{sec:hff} as a
parameter and is consumed by it, so what comes out is a probability, and $p =
10^{-19}$ means the same thing at six objectives as at nine or at nineteen
thousand. That is the whole point of reporting the angle as a probability rather
than as $\theta$, and it means a bar calibrated under one objective set
transfers to another unchanged. What did move is the angle itself. The bar was
set on a different seed and before the precision fix that dropped the
\texttt{f32} floor on a true law from $1.2\times10^{-10}$ to
$1.1\times10^{-14}$; four decades of headroom at the accurate end pushes every
real law far past $-19$, which is exactly the saturation we observe. The
solved and unsolved distributions then overlap because both sit deep in the tail,
not because the count of objectives changed the scale.

The consequence is a real one and the remedy is not recalibration. Re-setting
the bar per configuration would be treating a dimension-free quantity as though
it were not, and removing objectives to move $p$ would be deleting a measurement
to improve a number. If the condition is to earn its place it has to be at an
operating point where the angle discriminates --- which means asking which
objectives enter it, not how many. As it stands the error half of the bar,
strengthened on the train side (Section~\ref{sec:stopbar}), catches
\texttt{bacres2} --- the law $p$ was introduced for --- on a later false stop
and without consulting $p$ at all, and on this run $p$ adds nothing.

\textbf{What we have not tested.} The noisy tracks. The Feynman bonus set, where
we score 0 of 20 and always have. The nine problems of the
$1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ family, which have never been solved in any run on record
--- one of them, I.10.7, was found by a grafted wrap at generation 7 with test
$R^2 = 1.000000$ on one seed and not on another, which is a single event and not
a rate.

\section{Conclusion}

The result is 75 of 133 on one development seed at six minutes a fit, against a
published best of 54.1\% at ten seeds and up to eight hours. The measured change
that moved it is a selection restriction --- a tie-break in a tournament,
costing one \texttt{u32} per row and no additional dispatch, found by
instrumenting lineage and discovering that 294{,}000 fresh individuals had left
no descendants. What we think it is worth is broader than that, and stated as a
claim rather than a result: the restriction makes space in the population for
material that is not yet competitive, and a rewriter that converts back into the
genotype is what fills the space with proposals rather than with noise.
Section~\ref{sec:together} names the experiment that would separate the two, and
we have not run it.

The failure analysis is the more durable finding. Of the 67 laws unsolved before
this change, 53 were the search not reaching the law at all, and no amount of
work on how we write the answer touches those. The recoverable remainder is
concentrated in how the answer is written and when the search is allowed to
stop. Three cheap checks on our own output --- does the form survive
three-decimal rounding, is it finite on every row, is it short enough to parse
--- cover most of it, and the finiteness check held without exception across the
132 laws the scan completed.
